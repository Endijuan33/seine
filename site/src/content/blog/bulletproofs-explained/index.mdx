Bulletproofs are short zero-knowledge range proofs that help verify hidden payment amounts. This guide explains Pedersen commitments, why amounts need bounds, and how Bulletproofs stay small. In Blocknet, range proofs work alongside authorization, balance, repeated-spend and coinbase checks. Together these explain how ordinary payment privacy fits with validation of money movement and new issuance.

## What are Bulletproofs?

<Answer>
Bulletproofs are short zero-knowledge proofs, published by Bünz, Bootle, Boneh, Poelstra, Wuille and Maxwell in 2018. In cryptocurrencies they prove that a hidden amount lies in a valid range, such as 0 to 2^64−1, without revealing it. They need no trusted setup, and their size grows only with the logarithm of the range.
</Answer>

The paper, [Bulletproofs: Short Proofs for Confidential Transactions and More](https://eprint.iacr.org/2017/1066), appeared at the IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy in 2018. Its authors designed them for confidential transactions, where amounts on a blockchain are replaced by commitments. To see why such a proof is needed at all, start with the commitment.

## What is a Pedersen commitment?

A commitment is the cryptographic version of a sealed envelope. You put a number inside, show everyone the envelope, and can later prove what was inside. Nobody can read the number from the envelope, and you can't swap in a different number afterward.

A Pedersen commitment, described by Torben Pedersen in 1991, does this with elliptic-curve points. To commit to an amount `v`, a wallet picks a random secret `r`, called the blinding factor, and computes:

```
C = v·G + r·H
```

`G` and `H` are two fixed public points on the curve, chosen so that nobody knows how to write one as a multiple of the other. The result `C` is a single 32-byte point. It has two properties:

- **Hiding.** Because `r` is random, `C` looks like a random point. The same amount committed twice gives two unrelated commitments, and the commitment alone tells an observer nothing about `v`.
- **Binding.** Once `C` is published, the wallet can't open it to a different amount. Doing so would mean solving the discrete logarithm problem on the curve.

## How can nodes check a transaction they can't read?

<Answer>
Pedersen commitments can be added. The sum of two commitments is a commitment to the sum of their amounts, with the blinding factors added too. A node adds up a transaction's input commitments and its output commitments plus the fee, and checks the two totals match, so it confirms the balance without seeing any amount.
</Answer>

Adding two commitments gives `(v1 + v2)·G + (r1 + r2)·H`, which is itself a commitment to `v1 + v2`. The sender arranges the blinding factors so that the inputs’ total blinding equals the outputs’ total. If the amounts balance, subtracting the output commitments and the fee commitment from the input sum gives zero.

The network fee is public, alongside other transaction data such as the hash and input/output structure. Nodes turn the fee into a commitment with zero blinding and add it to the outputs side. For an ordinary payment, the conservation check is inputs = outputs + fee.

## Why do hidden amounts need range proofs?

Commitment arithmetic works modulo a very large number, the order of the curve group, which is about 2^252. In that arithmetic a "negative" amount is just a huge positive one. Someone could spend an input worth 10 coins and create two outputs, one worth 11 and one worth "minus 1". The commitments would balance perfectly, and 1 coin would appear from nothing, hidden from every observer.

A range proof closes that gap. It proves that the amount inside a commitment lies between 0 and 2^64−1 without saying what it is. With every output bounded that way, a handful of outputs can never add up to a number large enough to wrap around the group order, so balanced commitments mean balanced amounts.

Earlier confidential-transaction designs used range proofs that grew linearly with the number of bits. Bulletproofs grow only with the logarithm, which brings a 64-bit proof down to a few hundred bytes.

## How do Bulletproofs work?

A Bulletproof proves a range in three steps.

1. **Split the amount into bits.** A 64-bit amount becomes a vector of 64 bits. The prover commits to that vector and shows two facts about it: each entry is 0 or 1, and the bits, weighted by powers of two, add up to the committed amount.
2. **Turn those facts into one inner product.** Using random challenges, the prover folds all those conditions into a single claim about the inner product of two vectors. Because the challenges are random, a false claim passes only with negligible probability.
3. **Prove the inner product by halving.** Each round, the prover sends two curve points, `L` and `R`, and both vectors are folded to half their length. After log2(64) = 6 rounds, a single pair of numbers is left.

That halving is where the size comes from. The paper's abstract puts a range proof at `2·log2(n) + 9` group and field elements for an `n`-bit range. For 64 bits that is 12 points from the six rounds plus 9 more, 21 elements in total, or 672 bytes when each element is 32 bytes.

The proof is non-interactive. In place of a live verifier sending challenges, both sides derive them by hashing the proof transcript, a standard technique known as the Fiat-Shamir heuristic. Bulletproofs also need no trusted setup: the generators are public and derived openly, and security rests only on the discrete logarithm assumption. Several proofs can be aggregated, adding only a logarithmic number of elements, and verification can be batched.

## Who uses Bulletproofs?

Monero enabled Bulletproofs in its October 18, 2018 network upgrade (the v0.13.0 release) to reduce transaction sizes. The [Rust bulletproofs library](https://github.com/dalek-cryptography/bulletproofs) from the dalek-cryptography project implements them over the Ristretto group of Curve25519, and Blocknet's cryptography library is built on it.

## How does Blocknet validate private payments?

In released core v0.22.0, ordinary payment outputs carry Pedersen commitments, encrypted amounts and individual 64-bit Bulletproofs. Their amounts are hidden from the ordinary public record. The special coinbase transaction creates the block reward and has a separate, publicly checkable issuance path.

Full cryptographic validation of an ordinary payment includes these separate checks in the [released transaction validator](https://github.com/blocknetprivacy/core/blob/5a0a5b510c1dce46f4eeb12c33a291052e44e7b3/transaction.go#L960):

- **Authorization and binding.** Each input must use 16 canonical on-chain ring members. RingCT proves ownership and amount equality. The verifier also checks that the key image and pseudo-output commitment match the signed proof.
- **Range and balance.** Each output’s Bulletproof verifies against its commitment and bounds the amount to 0 through 2^64−1. The transcript uses the label "Blocknet Range Proof". Signed pseudo-outputs must sum to the outputs plus the fee. This checks conservation; it is not a replacement for authorization.
- **Repeated spending.** Key images must be distinct within the transaction and must not already be recorded as spent.

Checks **1–3** concern ordinary spending. Check **4** concerns the special transaction that creates the block reward. The diagram separates their roles; it does not depict a live transaction or replace the rest of block validation.

## How does the network detect a repeated spend?

A valid range proof says that an amount is within bounds. It does not say that the owner has not already spent the output. The key image supplies that separate linkability check without identifying the real ring member. Validation rejects duplicate images within one transaction and images already spent on the accepted chain. Binding the image to the signed RingCT proof prevents it from being swapped independently. [Who controls Blocknet](/blog/who-controls-blocknet/) explains how these rules relate to the nodes applying them.

## How is new issuance checked without reading wallet balances?

A block’s coinbase has no spending inputs. The [released block validator](https://github.com/blocknetprivacy/core/blob/5a0a5b510c1dce46f4eeb12c33a291052e44e7b3/block.go#L543) computes the permitted reward for the height and reconstructs the expected commitment and encoded amount using publicly derived blinding. Both must match. Its amount is therefore publicly derivable, unlike the hidden amounts of ordinary payments.

Cumulative issuance is computed by summing scheduled rewards from height 1; genesis has no coinbase. This total is not obtained by adding private wallet balances and is not a statement of how much is currently spendable or circulating. The [supply guide](/blog/blocknet-supply/) explains that distinction, while the [transaction walkthrough](/blog/blocknet-transaction-explained/) covers transfers and change that do not mint the reward again.

You can see this on the [Blocknet explorer](https://explorer.blocknetcrypto.com). A transaction page lists its amounts as "hidden · Pedersen commitments", and each output shows its commitment, an encrypted memo and a range proof of "672 bytes · Bulletproof", the size the math above predicts.

For ordinary payments, the recipient wallet recognizes its outputs and recovers their encrypted amounts with its private viewing information. The sender knows the amount entered. Payment proofs or viewing access shared deliberately can give other people additional information. These disclosure choices do not change the network’s commitment and proof checks.

Hidden ordinary payment amounts work with Blocknet’s other layers: [ring signatures](/blog/ring-signatures-explained/) over 16 outputs protect the spent source, [one-time stealth addresses](/blog/stealth-addresses-explained/) protect the receiving destination, and [Dandelion++](/blog/dandelion-plus-plus-explained/) reduces exposure of the network origin. The [privacy guide](/blog/blocknet-privacy-features/) explains each layer. These checks describe full validation; fast synchronization can rely on trusted historical checkpoints for some expensive signature and range-proof checks, as specified by the released implementation.
