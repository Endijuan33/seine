Paying someone should not require publishing your spending record to everyone who can read a blockchain. Blocknet applies its privacy mechanisms as part of ordinary wallet payments. This guide explains what the wallet protects automatically, how the network still checks a payment, and where your own records fit. Current behavior refers to released core v0.22.0; the Faraday section describes a proposal.

## What does private by default mean for a Blocknet payment?

<Answer>
Blocknet’s ordinary payments use ring signatures for spent inputs, one-time keys for recipients, and commitments with range proofs for amounts. Memos are encrypted, and Dandelion++ reduces exposure of the transaction’s network origin. The wallet applies these layers without a separate privacy switch, while public transaction structure and participants’ own records remain.
</Answer>

## How does the default protect the money you spend?

When you enter a recipient and amount, the wallet constructs a payment in the normal private format. You do not need to choose between a public transfer and a protected one. This makes privacy part of using BNT as currency: paying a person or business uses the same core protections as another ordinary payment. It also avoids making the decision to enable privacy a distinguishing choice.

Each normal input uses a CLSAG ring of 16 outputs: one real output you own and 15 decoys from the chain. The [released validation rules](https://github.com/blocknetprivacy/core/blob/5a0a5b510c1dce46f4eeb12c33a291052e44e7b3/transaction.go#L1013) enforce that size and require real on-chain ring members. The signature proves that the spend is authorized without identifying which member supplied it. Decoys provide possible sources for the proof; they do not fund your payment. A key image lets the network detect repeated spending. The [ring-signature guide](/blog/ring-signatures-explained/) explains the mechanism.

## How are the recipient and payment note protected?

Your receiving address lets a payer construct an output for you. The wallet derives a one-time output key rather than placing that familiar receiving address beside every payment on the chain. Your wallet uses its private viewing information to recognize incoming outputs. You can reuse a receiving address while the payments use their own output keys. The [stealth-address guide](/blog/stealth-addresses-explained/) explains that relationship.

A public [Blocknet ID name](/blog/blocknet-id-pay-by-name/) provides a convenient way to find the receiving destination. Someone may associate the published name with you, but an ordinary payment to that destination still uses the same output construction. Publishing a receiving name does not publish the wallet’s private viewing keys.

You can also attach an invoice reference or a short note. The wallet encrypts the memo using a shared secret for that output. The memo field occupies 128 bytes and supports up to 124 bytes of text; shorter or empty content receives random padding before encryption. Its fixed field size therefore does not announce the note’s length. Sender and recipient can know the note, and deliberately shared verification or viewing information can disclose it. The [memo guide](/blog/blocknet-encrypted-memos/) covers writing and reading notes.

## How can the network check hidden amounts?

Ordinary payment outputs carry Pedersen commitments instead of publishing their amounts as readable values. Nodes check that the transaction balances, including its public fee, through these commitments and the associated proofs. Bulletproofs range proofs establish that output amounts are within the permitted range. Together, the checks validate the money movement while keeping the payment amounts out of the ordinary public record.

Your wallet can still show the amount you receive. The [range-proof guide](/blog/bulletproofs-explained/) explains the cryptography, while the [transaction walkthrough](/blog/blocknet-transaction-explained/) follows selected inputs, recipient outputs and change. Change uses new outputs belonging to the sending wallet; privacy does not remove the need to account for what was spent and what returned.

Public records retain a transaction hash, input and output structure, the fee, and block status when included. A private payment therefore remains a verifiable transaction. This explanation concerns ordinary spending: the special coinbase transaction that creates a block reward has no spending inputs and follows separate validation rules. Distinguishing those cases keeps “private by default” tied to the payment behavior it describes.

## What happens beyond the public transaction?

[Dandelion++](/blog/dandelion-plus-plus-explained/) addresses network relay. A new transaction first travels along a stem before wider broadcast, reducing the direct connection between its origin and the peers that announce it broadly. It is an additional layer alongside the transaction cryptography. It does not promise to conceal an IP address in every network situation.

The people participating in a payment also have records. A sender knows the destination and amount entered; the recipient recognizes their receipt. An invoice, conversation or shared screenshot can connect those details to a person or order. That outside information is separate from what the transaction format publishes. The [payment-visibility guide](/blog/blocknet-payment-visibility/) compares public, sender, recipient and granted viewing access, including deliberate payment proofs. For ordinary currency use, keep the distinction clear: the network validates the transfer while each participant retains the details needed for their own accounting.

## What would the proposed Faraday upgrade change?

The [official October 27 explanation](https://blocknetcrypto.com/blog/2026-10-27-how-blocknet-privacy-survives-quantum-computers/) describes Faraday as a design still being built and tested. Faraday would replace the current spending proof with hash-based proofs covering the full set of Faraday coins. That set would grow as coins enter the new format; it does not mean every historical output is already included. The Faraday proposal would use ML-KEM for receiving and payment detection, a separate job from proving a spend.

The [Faraday privacy guide](/blog/blocknet-faraday-privacy/) explains those planned mechanisms. The proposal’s design and timing can change, and current wallet behavior should be read from the released rules until the applicable upgrade. For currency use today, privacy by default means the ordinary payment format protects sensitive details without an extra mode to select. Its relationship to interchangeable units is explained in [Blocknet and fungibility](/blog/blocknet-fungibility/); practical sending steps remain in the [payment guide](/blog/send-receive-blocknet/).
